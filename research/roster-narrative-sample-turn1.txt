The bottle is cold in your hand, and when you tip it the water comes out flat and clear, smelling of nothing at all. You drink before you have decided to. Your mouth is dry enough that the water passes over your tongue with no taste you could name.

You lower it, and what runs down your chin and into your collar. The wind takes the cold off that. Eight degrees, a light wind, a strip of damp gravel under your boots — nothing here is dangerous on its own — but an hour of standing still on a ridge has put a fine tremor into your forearms, and you notice it only now that you have stopped moving.

You set the bottle back down on the gravel where you found it, tipped over on its side, a little short of full. The cap is still off it. Nobody else has been up this trail: the tread is packed flat and old, and the path runs a single line down the ridge and stops being a path where the white begins.

You look down.

The descent is one long steep drop, no switchbacks in it, no way to hold a line except straight. The fog comes up the slope to meet it and takes the last stretch of trail, and it does not take it gradually. There is a place where the gravel ends, and then there is white, and the two do not grade into each other at all. Past that there is no far ridge, no tree, no landmark of any kind — nothing to tell you how wide it is. The fog goes out past the horizon and never lifts.

The wind is still here, combing the grass on the uphill side flat and letting it up again. The fog does not move. Not a ripple, not a seam, not one slow shoulder rolling under the slope where the light hits it. It sits the way something poured sits — except that it goes past the horizon, and the top of it lies below you, and the light lies flat on it the way light lies on water.

Nothing crosses the white. No shadow travels through it. If anything is standing in that fog it is not saying so, and from up here you have no way of finding out.

And there is the drop on your cheek — at the corner of your jaw, wet and red, cool against the skin, about the size of a grain of rice. You turn your head to put it in the light and look. The skin around it is unbroken: no scratch, no cut, nothing under it to account for anything. The drop has not run anywhere. It has not dried. It is simply there, and nothing has explained it, and you have no explanation ready either.

You stand at the trailhead with a tipped bottle at your feet and an hour of cold in your hands. Above you the switchbacks climb away from the fog line, into morning. Below you the descent ends in white. The trail is empty. You are the only thing on it.
